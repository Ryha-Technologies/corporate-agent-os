# Source evaluation checklist

Apply to every source before it enters the evidence ledger.

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Who published it, and what do they gain from the claim? | Vendor marketing, funded reports and advocacy groups are not independent validation. |
| When was it published or updated, and does the date matter for this claim? | Prices, laws, APIs and market shares go stale within months. |
| Does the page actually say what the summary claims? Open it. | Search snippets and AI summaries misattribute routinely. |
| Is the underlying data described (sample, method, geography)? | A statistic without method is an opinion with a number. |
| Is it primary (filing, statute, docs, dataset) or a retelling? | Follow retellings to their source; cite the source. |
| Is there a contradicting source of equal or better quality? | Record it. The ledger needs the disagreement, not a winner picked silently. |
| Does it cover the user's geography and segment? | US SaaS pricing says little about Indian SMEs. |
| Is it reachable later (stable URL, archive)? | Record access date; capture key figures in the ledger so the claim survives link rot. |

## Source types and how far to trust them for a consequential claim

| Type | Use |
|---|---|
| Official filings, statutes, regulator guidance, standards, product documentation, pricing pages, public datasets | Primary evidence. Still check dates and scope. |
| Peer-reviewed research, audited disclosures, recognised industry bodies | Strong evidence; check method match. |
| Named-source journalism and specialist analysis | Supporting evidence; follow to primary where possible. |
| Forums, reviews, social posts | Qualitative signals: language, frustrations, workarounds. Never prevalence. |
| Unsourced blogs, SEO pages, generated summaries, slide decks without references | Leads only. Never the sole support for a consequential claim. |

## Traps

- Circular sourcing: three articles citing one unverified press release.
- Survivorship: only successful companies are visible in listicles.
- Recency bias: last month's funding news treated as structural change.
- Search volume as demand: people search for problems they will not pay to solve.
- Benchmark headlines without setup: a model "wins" on a task unlike the workload.
